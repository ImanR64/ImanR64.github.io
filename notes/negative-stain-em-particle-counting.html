Counting particles in negative-stain EM automatically: a practical workflow

Counting particles by eye across hundreds of micrographs is slow and hard to reproduce. A simple automated workflow fixes most of this, without a full cryo-EM software stack.

By Dr. Iman Rostami · 25 November 2026 · 5 min read

Negative-stain electron microscopy is quick and inexpensive, which makes it the first look at almost every purified complex. Counting the particles, however, is often still done by eye. That is slow and tiring, and two people rarely count the same micrograph the same way. The six steps below make counts faster, reproducible and defensible in peer review.

1. Collect data with counting in mind

  • Use the same magnification, defocus and stain for every condition you compare.
  • Take micrographs from several grid squares, not from one favourable area.
  • Plan for at least 20–30 good micrographs per condition, and the same number for controls.
  • Record the pixel size: every size rule later depends on it.

2. Clean up the images

Correct uneven background, for example by subtracting a strongly blurred copy of the image, then denoise lightly and normalise contrast so that all micrographs look alike to the algorithm.

3. Detect particles

For roughly round particles, a blob detector (Laplacian of Gaussian) tuned to the expected particle size works well. For complex shapes, a small deep-learning model trained on a few hand-labelled micrographs is more robust. Always look at the detections overlaid on the image before trusting any number.

4. Classify what you found

Purified samples are rarely pure. Use size and shape rules in nanometres, such as diameter and elongation, to separate species, and distance rules to find pairs or clusters. Exclude stain artefacts, contamination and overlapping particles explicitly, so that the rules are written down rather than kept in someone's head.

5. Check quality

Label a few micrographs by hand and compare them with the automatic result. If agreement is poor, adjust the settings and rerun. This step is what makes the numbers defensible to reviewers.

6. Analyse the counts correctly

The micrograph, not the particle, is the unit of observation, and micrographs from the same grid are not independent. Report counts or fractions per micrograph, show their spread, and use a statistical model that accounts for grids or preparations, such as a mixed model.

Comparing pooled particle totals between conditions overstates certainty. Thousands of particles from two grids are still two grids.

Tools

All of this can be done in Python with scikit-image and napari, in Fiji, or in a dedicated desktop tool. I built such a tool, RiboSort, to detect and sort ribosomal subunits in negative-stain micrographs, and I develop similar tools for other particle types and imaging methods.
